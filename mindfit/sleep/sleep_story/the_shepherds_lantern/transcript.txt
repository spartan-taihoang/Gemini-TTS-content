Good evening. Tonight's story is called The Shepherd's Lantern. Before we begin, let your shoulders drop... and let the bed hold all of your weight.
Take one slow breath in... and let it go. There is nowhere you need to be now. Nothing left to fix tonight. Just listen, and let the story carry you.
Far away, in a land of soft brown hills, there lived an old shepherd named Eli. His house was small and made of stone, with one window that looked toward the east. And every evening, when the sun went down behind the hills, Eli took his lantern from its hook beside the door.
It was a plain lantern. Iron, a little rusted at the edges, with a glass that had been cleaned so many times it had gone faintly cloudy. But it gave a warm, honey-colored light... and the sheep knew it the way children know their mother's voice.
On this particular evening, the air was cool and still. The last of the swallows had gone to their nests under the roof. Somewhere down in the valley, a dog barked once, and then thought better of it. Eli lit the wick with a small brass match, closed the little glass door, and stepped outside.
The path to the high pasture was narrow and familiar. He had walked it for more than fifty years, and his feet found every stone without needing to look. The grass brushed against his ankles, damp with the first of the evening dew. It smelled of thyme, and of earth, and of the long warm day now ending.
As he walked, Eli hummed an old song his father had taught him. It had no real words... only a slow rising and falling, like breathing. Up ahead, he could hear the sheep. A soft bleat here. The small bell on the lead ewe, ringing once, then again.
At the top of the hill, the pasture opened wide beneath the sky. The sheep were gathered close together near the old stone wall, pale shapes in the deepening blue. When they saw the lantern coming, they lifted their heads... and then, one by one, they lowered them again. He was here. All was well.
Eli counted them, as he always did. Not quickly, but slowly, touching each one with his eyes. Thirty-one, thirty-two, thirty-three... all of them, every one. Even the little brown lamb who liked to wander was there, tucked against her mother's side.
He set the lantern on a flat rock by the wall, where its light could spread across the grass. Then he sat down on his old wool cloak, leaned his back against the cool stones, and rested his staff across his knees.
Above him, the stars were coming out. First one, low in the west, bright as a drop of silver. Then a few more. Then too many to count. The sky seemed to grow deeper and wider, the way it only does in places far from any town.
Eli had often wondered how the One who made all those stars could also know the name of one old shepherd on one small hill. And yet he believed it. He had felt it, on nights like this one.
He remembered the words his mother used to say to him at bedtime, when he was a small boy afraid of the dark. I will both lay me down in peace, and sleep: for thou, Lord, only makest me dwell in safety.
A breeze moved through the pasture, soft as a hand passing over the grass. The lantern flame leaned, and then stood straight again. One of the sheep sighed in her sleep. Far below, in the village, the last window went dark.
Eli watched over them, as the night grew quieter. He listened to the small sounds of the hillside. The crickets in the wall. The slow chewing of a ewe who was not quite ready to sleep. The distant whisper of the stream at the bottom of the valley.
He was not worried. He had done what a shepherd does. He had walked the path. He had counted his flock. He had set his light where they could see it. And now there was nothing left to do but to stay close, and keep watch, and rest.
And perhaps you are a little like those sheep tonight. Gathered in. Counted. Known by name. You do not have to keep watch over the night yourself. Someone kinder and stronger than any shepherd is already watching.
As the hours passed, the lantern burned lower. Its light grew smaller and softer, until it was only a small golden glow on the rock. Eli's eyes grew heavy. He pulled his cloak a little closer around his shoulders.
The stars turned slowly overhead, the way they had turned for a thousand years. The grass was silver now with dew. The little brown lamb was fast asleep. Her mother was asleep. The whole hillside was breathing slowly, all together, like one great quiet creature resting under the sky.
And Eli, the old shepherd, let his head rest back against the stones. He thought of nothing in particular. The warmth of the cloak. The smell of the grass. The light, still glowing, small and faithful, beside him.
Let yourself be there with him now. The cool air on your face. The soft ground beneath you. The stars above, and the quiet all around. Breathe in slowly... and breathe out.
