In the tenth chapter of John's Gospel, Jesus speaks in pictures his first listeners would have understood immediately.
He talks about sheep and shepherds, about doors and pastures, about safety and danger.
But woven through these familiar images is something that goes deeper than any comparison.
Jesus is telling us who he is, and he is telling us that he knows who we are.
The passage begins with Jesus calling himself the door of the sheep.
Then said Jesus unto them again, Verily, verily, I say unto you, I am the door of the sheep.
A door is the one point of access, the single way in and the single way out.
In the world Jesus knew, a shepherd would sometimes sleep across the opening of the fold at night, his own body serving as the gate.
Nothing could come in or go out without passing by him.
Jesus places himself in that position for his own.
He says that all who came before him were thieves and robbers.
He is speaking of false messiahs, of teachers who promise rescue but deliver only harm.
The true sheep did not listen to them because something in those voices did not ring true.
Then Jesus makes a promise.
I am the door: by me if any man enter in, he shall be saved, and shall go in and out, and find pasture.
To enter by him means to come to God through him, not through your own merit or effort or spiritual achievement.
And the promise is safety and freedom together.
You shall be saved, and you shall go in and out and find pasture.
The thief comes only to steal and kill and destroy.
But Jesus says he came that his sheep might have life, and have it more abundantly.
This abundance is not wealth or comfort.
It is the fullness of life that comes from being known and loved by God.
Then the image shifts slightly.
I am the good shepherd: the good shepherd giveth his life for the sheep.
A hired hand works for wages and runs when danger comes, because the sheep are not his own.
But the good shepherd owns the flock, and the flock belongs to him, and he does not abandon them.
He lays down his life.
Jesus is not speaking theoretically.
When he spoke these words, the cross was already ahead of him, and he knew it.
He was describing what he was about to do.
And then he speaks the words that gather everything together.
I am the good shepherd, and know my sheep, and am known of mine.
As the Father knoweth me, even so know I the Father: and I lay down my life for the sheep.
To be known by Christ is not to be observed from a distance or assessed by a set of standards.
It is to be known in the way the Father knows the Son and the Son knows the Father.
It is an intimate, unbreakable knowing, the kind of knowing that holds nothing back.
Jesus knows your name.
He knows the weight you have carried today and the thoughts that have circled in your mind as you lie here in the dark.
He knows what you regret and what you fear.
He knows the places where you have failed and the places where you have tried and found yourself too weak.
And his knowing is not the knowing of a judge looking for fault.
It is the knowing of a shepherd who has already decided to lay down his life.
You do not have to wonder tonight whether you are seen.
You do not have to work to get his attention or prove that you deserve his care.
You are already known, completely and without condition.
The door is not closed to you.
The door is a person, and that person is Christ, and he has opened the way to the Father.
As you rest now, let that truth settle around you.
You are one of his sheep, and he knows your voice, and you are safe.
